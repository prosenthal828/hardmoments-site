What to do when parents disagree on discipline

You say no screens before homework. Your partner says one show is fine. Before long, the kids learn that a "no" from one parent is a reason to go ask the other.

Disagreeing about discipline is normal. You grew up in different homes, with different rules. You don't have to agree on everything. You only have to agree on enough that your kids hear one answer. Here is a simple way to get there.

1. Talk about it away from the kids

Arguing about a rule in front of the child who broke it turns a small moment into a contest, and the child ends up as the referee. Save the real conversation for a calm time: after bedtime, on a walk, or over coffee. Right after a blowup is the worst time. If one of you is still upset, wait a day.

Start with what you each want for your kids, not with who was wrong last night. Most parents want the same things, like a child who is safe, kind and able to hear "no." You usually differ on how to get there, not on where you're going.

2. Agree on two or three non-negotiables

Don't try to write a rulebook. Pick the few things you will both hold firm on every time, no matter who is home. For many families that means safety (car seats, no hitting, holding hands in parking lots), basic respect (no name-calling), and one routine that keeps falling apart, such as bedtime.

Everything else can bend. It's fine if one of you tolerates a messier playroom. Small differences between parents are part of life. The trouble starts when the big rules depend on who happens to be in the room.

3. Find a middle path for the rest

Take one recurring fight at a time. Each of you says what you would do and why, while the other just listens. Then look for the part you already share.

Say one of you wants a firm early bedtime and the other wants slack on weekends. A middle path might be lights out at 7:30 on school nights and 8:30 on Fridays and Saturdays, with the same two-book routine every night.

A middle path doesn't mean splitting every difference down the middle. Sometimes one parent's idea simply fits better, and the other agrees to try it for two weeks and then talk again.

4. Write it down

Memory is kind to whoever is doing the remembering. A short written plan ends the "I never agreed to that" argument. Keep it to a few lines for each situation:

  • What usually happens
  • What we'll say (keep it short)
  • What we'll do
  • What we'll do if it gets worse

Put it somewhere you'll both see it, like the fridge or a shared note on your phones.

5. Back each other up in the moment

When your partner is handling something, let them finish, even if you'd do it differently. Unless someone is unsafe, the time to disagree is later, in private. Overruling each other in front of the kids teaches them to shop for the softer answer.

If you need to step in, offer help instead of a correction. "Want me to take over?" is much easier to hear than "That's not what we said."

A short script for the moment

Use this when you don't like how a moment is going but you want to stay on the same side.

To your partner, calmly: "I'm with you. Let's finish this and talk after."

To your child: "We're on the same team. The answer is no for now."

Later, just the two of you: "Can we talk about dinner? I wasn't sure about the timeout. What were you seeing? Here's what I was worried about."

If you decide to change course: "We talked, and we're changing the plan. From now on, ..."

That last line matters. When a rule changes, the kids should hear it from both of you, so it doesn't sound like one parent won.

6. Review it every week

Kids grow, schedules change, and a plan that worked in September may not fit in March. Set aside ten minutes once a week (Sunday night works for a lot of couples) and ask three questions:

  1. What worked this week?
  2. Where did we disagree?
  3. What, if anything, should we change?

Update the written plan, and keep the changes small so the kids can keep up.

When to get more help

If arguments about discipline keep turning into bigger fights, or you're worried about your child's behavior or safety, talk to your child's doctor or a licensed family counselor. This page is general information for parents. It isn't therapy or medical advice. If a child is in danger, call 911.
